# Appendix A. Two phase effects in learning a magnetic field through a gyrating particle

This appendix derives the two results that govern training in the main text: the systematic gyro-phase deficit of the standard Boris pusher and its consequence for the learned field (A.1), and the phase-wrapping condition that limits the length of a multiple-shooting segment (A.2). Both derivations are deliberately elementary. Their purpose is to explain the measured behaviour and to give a usable rule, not to provide sharp bounds, and we state where the assumptions break down.

Throughout, lengths are measured in Earth radii, speeds in units of the particle speed, and time in $R_E/|v|$. The equation of motion is $\dot{\mathbf v} = (s/\varepsilon)\,\mathbf v\times\mathbf B(\mathbf r)$, with $s=-1$ for electrons and $\varepsilon = r_L/R_E = 0.02$, so the local gyro-frequency is $\Omega(\mathbf r) = |\mathbf B(\mathbf r)|/\varepsilon$.

## A.1 Gyro-phase deficit of the Boris rotation and its imprint on the learned field

In a locally uniform field the Boris update rotates the velocity about $\hat{\mathbf b}$ using the vector $\mathbf t = (s/\varepsilon)\,\mathbf B\,\Delta t/2$, whose magnitude is $\Omega\Delta t/2$. The rotation it produces is exact in the sense that $|\mathbf v|$ is preserved, but its angle is not $\Omega\Delta t$. The standard construction (rotation through the half-angle tangent) gives

$$\theta_{\mathrm{B}} = 2\arctan\!\left(\frac{\Omega\Delta t}{2}\right) = \Omega\Delta t\left[1 - \frac{(\Omega\Delta t)^2}{12} + O\!\left((\Omega\Delta t)^4\right)\right],$$

so the discrete particle gyrates slower than the physical one, with relative frequency deficit $\eta = (\Omega\Delta t)^2/12$. At the model step used in this work, $\Delta t = 0.004$, the equatorial value $\Omega\Delta t \approx 0.2$ gives $\eta = 3.31\times10^{-3}$ (the series value $3.33\times10^{-3}$ is accurate to better than one per cent here, and to about four per cent at $\Omega\Delta t = 0.5$). Over 50 gyrations this amounts to a phase loss of $2\pi\cdot 50\cdot\eta = 1.04$ rad, which is exactly what the integrator produces numerically in a uniform field.

The deficit matters for learning because the data loss is dominated by gyro-phase: a position error of one gyroradius corresponds to a phase error of order one radian. A model that integrates with the standard Boris step and is asked to reproduce the observed phase can only do so by increasing $|\mathbf B|$ by the same fraction, $\delta B/B \approx \eta$. The learned field therefore absorbs the integrator error. This is invisible on the training data but distorts quantities that depend on the field rather than on the phase, in particular the mirror ratio $B(s)/B(s_0)$ along a field line, the mirror points, and hence $J$. In the experiments the field-aligned error of an otherwise exact harmonic basis was 0.45 per cent of $|\mathbf B|$, close to $\eta = 0.33$ per cent, and the relative $J$ error was 0.62 per cent.

The correction is to replace the half-angle tangent by the exact one, $\mathbf t \to \tan(\Omega\Delta t/2)\,\hat{\mathbf t}$, which makes the rotation angle equal to $\Omega\Delta t$ in a locally uniform field at no additional cost. The phase error over 50 gyrations then falls to $4\times10^{-14}$ rad, the field-aligned error of the harmonic model to 0.08 per cent, and its $J$ error to 0.19 per cent. For a flexible network the correction gives no gain, because there the dominant error comes from how the network extrapolates the field (Section 7 of the analysis). We therefore recommend the exact-phase rotation as the default inside any training loop that fits gyro-phase.

## A.2 The phase-wrapping condition for multiple shooting

Consider one segment of length $T_s$ that starts from a node state and is integrated with the current model field $\mathbf B_\theta$. Write the field-strength mismatch along the true path as $|\mathbf B_\theta| = |\mathbf B|\,(1-\delta(t))$, with $|\delta|\ll1$. To first order the guiding-centre motion is unaffected over a segment that is short compared with the bounce period. The gyro-phase, however, drifts at the rate $\Omega(t)\delta(t)$, so the accumulated mismatch at time $\tau$ into the segment is

$$\varphi(\tau) = \int_0^{\tau}\Omega(t)\,\delta(t)\,dt, \qquad \Delta\varphi \equiv \varphi(T_s) = \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\text{segment}}\big(|\mathbf B| - |\mathbf B_\theta|\big)\,dt .$$

The position residual of the predicted gyration relative to the true one has magnitude $2\rho\,|\sin(\varphi/2)|$, where $\rho$ is the gyroradius, and the squared residual is proportional to $1-\cos\varphi$. Two terms of the objective respond to this. The continuity defect at the segment end is proportional to $1-\cos\Delta\varphi$. Its derivative with respect to the field scale has the correct, error-reducing sign only for $|\Delta\varphi|<\pi$; beyond this the gradient pushes the phase towards the next minimum at $2\pi$, which corresponds to a wrong field. The data term sums residuals along the segment. For a constant mismatch rate its segment average is $1-\sin x/x$ with $x = \Delta\varphi$. This function increases monotonically up to $x^\ast = 4.493$, the first root of $\tan x = x$ (that is, $1.43\pi$), and is convex only up to $x\approx2.08$. The data term alone would therefore tolerate somewhat longer segments than the continuity term.

The usable condition follows from requiring that the initial model lie inside the basin of the correct field on every segment. If the initial model is the known dipole, then $\delta(t)$ is the relative contribution of the unknown perturbation to $|\mathbf B|$, and the condition reads

$$\Delta\varphi_0 = \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\text{segment}}\big(|\mathbf B_{\text{true}}| - |\mathbf B_{\text{dipole}}|\big)\,dt \;\lesssim\; x_c, \qquad \pi \le x_c \le 4.49 .$$

For a constant relative error $\delta$ this becomes a limit on the number of gyrations per segment, $N = \Omega T_s/2\pi$:

$$N < N_c = \frac{x_c}{2\pi\delta} \;\approx\; \frac{1}{2\delta}\quad\text{for } x_c=\pi,$$

which is the form used in Section 3 of the analysis. Equivalently, a trajectory of duration $T$ needs at least $K \gtrsim T\,\bar\Omega\,\bar\delta/x_c$ segments.

Three features of the real problem are not captured by this argument. The node states are themselves trainable, so part of an initial phase error can be absorbed by shifting the node gyro-phase rather than the field. The mismatch $\delta(t)$ is not constant: off the equator $|\mathbf B|$ is larger and the relative perturbation smaller. And the observations are noisy. For these reasons the threshold $x_c$ is an empirical constant within the interval set by the two bounds, and the equatorial form $N_c = 1/(2\delta)$ is an estimate of order of magnitude.

The experiments support the condition in both forms. With $\delta$ taken as the equatorial value $b_s/(1-b_s)$, the rule $N<N_c$ classified the outcome of 48 of 52 training runs across three storm strengths. All four misclassified runs lay in the transition band $0.84\le N/N_c\le1.04$. When $\Delta\varphi_0$ is instead computed directly from the definition above, as the median over the uniform segments of the noise-free training trajectories with no free parameter, a single threshold separates convergence from failure in 51 of 52 runs. The transition is at $\Delta\varphi_0\approx2.8$ rad, about $0.9\pi$, and is the same for the three storm strengths: the weak storm with $K=16$ converges at $\Delta\varphi_0=2.80$ rad, while the moderate storm with $K=32$ fails at $2.84$ rad. The observed threshold lies at the lower, continuity-term end of the interval, which suggests that the endpoint defect, rather than the averaged data term, determines when the shooting problem loses its correct basin.

The threshold was fixed on runs that used the standard Boris rotation, and we then tested it on 36 new runs that used the exact-phase rotation of A.1, again with three storm strengths and four segment counts around the bound. Without any adjustment, the same value of 2.83 rad classified 35 of these 36 runs correctly. The boundary falls between the same two configurations as before, and the only misclassified case is again the strong storm with $K=80$ ($\Delta\varphi_0=2.31$ rad), where one of three seeds stalls partially. Over all 88 runs the criterion is correct in 97.7 per cent of cases, against 84 per cent if $\pi$ itself is used as the threshold. The value is, however, not universal to the second digit. For the asymmetric ring-current field of the main text, where $\delta(t)$ varies strongly along the path, a network trained with $K=24$ converged in three of three seeds although its median mismatch was $\Delta\varphi_0 = 2.92$ rad, three per cent above the threshold. We therefore quote the condition as $\Delta\varphi_0 \lesssim 2.8$–$2.9$ rad, or about $0.9\pi$.

The condition bounds the segment length from above only. The experiments also show a lower limit of a different nature. When segments contain about one gyration or less ($K=96$ for the moderate storm), the training loss falls to the noise level, but the error on unseen electrons grows by 10 to 34 per cent. Each segment introduces six free node parameters that can absorb error, and the field is then less constrained by the data. We do not derive this limit here. In practice it argues for choosing $K$ just below the phase-wrapping bound, or for a curriculum that starts with short segments while $\delta$ is large and merges them as $\delta$ decreases. The curriculum used in the main text reaches the accuracy of the best hand-chosen $K$ without knowing $\delta$ in advance.
